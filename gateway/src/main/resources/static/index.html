How it works

Every ping is an event.

A simulated bus sends its position every few seconds. Four small services turn those pings into a live map, with Kafka in the middle so each piece can be scaled or replaced on its own.

SimulatorBuses drive real BMTC routes: they brake for stops and red signals, dwell, and get stuck in jams.
→
ETA serviceReads bus.positions, learns how each bus runs against its timetable, and flags trouble.
→
GatewayReads bus.eta and bus.alerts, then streams them to browsers over SSE.

This mapSearch a stop, plan a direct ride, or watch a single bus.
01

One bus, one order

Positions are keyed by vehicle ID, so every ping from a bus lands on the same partition, in order. Several ETA instances split the buses between them as one consumer group.

02

An ETA that learns the bus

Each finished stop-to-stop segment shows how fast this bus is running against its timetable. The rest of the trip is scaled by that pace, and the error is tracked against a timetable-only baseline.

03

Trouble gets noticed

A bus crawling below 7 km/h away from a stop raises a delay alert. A bus that goes silent becomes a ghost bus. Duplicate and out-of-order pings are dropped.

What's real

Routes, stops, stop sequences and timetables, from the unofficial BMTC GTFS dataset by Vonter (scraped from the Namma BMTC app, so it has rough edges).

What's simulated

GPS positions, traffic and incidents. BMTC doesn't publish a public real-time feed. The producer is the only piece that would change when one exists.
